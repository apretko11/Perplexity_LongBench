ATTENTION DROPOUT: TASK-LEVEL LONGBENCH RESULTS (16K)

SUMMARY

The most interesting result is the 906M model trained and evaluated at 16K with attention dropout 0.2. Compared with its zero-dropout counterpart, it improves perplexity on several retrieval and QA tasks: Passage Retrieval (−71.14%), TriviaQA (−33.74%), Musique (−33.63%), and NarrativeQA (−29.35%). Its worst five tasks exhibit much smaller degradations, with Passage Count increasing by 7.08%. This helps explain why the 906M model improves both pooled and task-balanced perplexity.

The 271M and 504M results are considerably less consistent. Both models benefit on certain tasks, but suffer substantial degradation on others. For example, the 271M model improves MultifieldQA (−11.52%) while worsening Passage Count (+263.74%) and TREC (+161.92%). The 504M model improves Passage Retrieval (−41.70%) and Musique (−35.26%), but worsens TREC (+159.54%). These uneven effects help explain why dropout's benefits can disappear—or reverse—when we give each LongBench task equal weight.

Pooled perplexity weights each task by its gold-answer token count; task-balanced perplexity assigns equal weight to all 16 tasks in per-token negative log-likelihood space. Short-answer tasks can therefore matter much more to the balanced metric. These are descriptive results from five examples per task, evaluating the first reference answer rather than free-generation accuracy.

tl;dr

Attention dropout changes performance unevenly across tasks, and the distribution of those effects depends strongly on model architecture.

1. Moderate attention dropout can improve long-context gold-answer perplexity, particularly for 906M models. The 906M model trained and evaluated at 16K shows improvements under both pooled and task-balanced metrics.
2. The effect is strongly architecture- and task-dependent. The smaller models show substantial degradation on certain tasks, including TREC and Passage Count. In contrast, the 906M model shows large improvements across several retrieval and QA tasks.
3. The aggregation method materially changes the conclusions. Token-weighted perplexity and equal-task-weighted perplexity can disagree because dropout's effects are unevenly distributed across tasks.

QUALIFICATIONS:

1. These comparisons use just five examples per task, so very large task-level percentage changes need cautious interpretation.
2. A +264% increase in perplexity on one task is dramatic, but isn't necessarily a correspondingly large contribution to overall perplexity.
3. These are matched-checkpoint-family comparisons, not evidence of improved generation accuracy.
